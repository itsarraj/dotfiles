Long before the modern Olympics, Greek athletes were already solving the problem most performers never crack.

Not weak muscles. Not missing talent. Fear.

Specifically, the fear of failure and what it would mean about who they were.

So they developed a mental ritual called prokatalēpsis. And it worked so effectively that it was later considered to give an unfair competitive advantage.

The night before competition the athlete would do something that sounds counterintuitive. He would imagine losing. Fully, clearly, and without excuses. Not to discourage himself. Not to spiral into doubt. But to drain fear of the power it holds over the unprepared mind. Because the Greeks understood something modern psychology has since confirmed — you cannot be genuinely afraid of what you have already fully accepted.

Then came the second part. Standing alone in silence, the athlete repeated one sentence until his body believed it. What remains after fear is my true form.
They believed this single practice revealed the identity that exists beneath ego, expectation, and the imagined judgment of others. Athletes slept deeper. Moved more freely. Competed with a calm that felt almost untouchable to those around them.

Modern neuroscience calls this exposure reconsolidation. The Greeks called it returning to yourself before the world interferes.

Next time fear tightens your chest, accept the worst outcome for sixty seconds. Then say the line.

Something very old may stir awake inside you.
